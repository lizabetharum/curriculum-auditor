# Measuring heights you cannot reach

This lesson is synthetic. It was written for this repository as test material for the curriculum auditor, and its answer key contains planted errors. Do not use it in a classroom as written.

## Overview

What it is: one 50-minute lesson on right-triangle trigonometry. Students use sine, cosine, and tangent to find a length they cannot measure directly.

Who it is for: Algebra 2 or geometry students who have learned the three ratios but have not yet used them outside a worksheet.

How it works: a warm-up estimate, two tasks, and an exit ticket. Task A is individual work on a ramp. Task B is partner work outdoors (or in a hallway with a tall wall). All angles in this lesson are in degrees. Set calculators to degree mode before the warm-up.

Materials: a phone clinometer app or a paper clinometer, a 30-meter tape measure, graph paper, and a calculator.

## Warm-up

5 minutes.

Look at the tallest tree or building you can see from the classroom window. Write down your best guess for its height in meters. Write one sentence about how you made the guess. Keep this page. You will compare it to your measured answer in Task B.

## Task A: The ramp

15 minutes, on your own.

The robotics team built a ramp to roll their cart onto the stage. The ramp board is 3.6 meters long. It makes a 5° angle with the floor.

1. Draw the ramp as a right triangle. Label the ramp length, the angle, the height you want to find, and the flat distance along the floor.
2. Choose sine, cosine, or tangent. Write one sentence explaining why that ratio fits the sides you know.
3. Find the height of the stage, to the nearest centimeter.
4. Check your height a second way. You could find the flat distance and use the Pythagorean theorem, make a scale drawing on graph paper, or compare with an estimate. Show the check.
5. Our triangle treats the ramp as a perfectly straight board on a perfectly flat floor. Name one way the real ramp could differ from the triangle, and say how it would change your answer.

## Task B: Something tall outside

20 minutes, with a partner.

Pick the tree or building from the warm-up. Stand where you can see its top and its base.

1. Measure the distance from your feet to the base with the tape measure.
2. Use the clinometer to measure the angle of elevation from your eye to the top.
3. Measure the height of your eye above the ground.
4. Find the height of the tree or building. Show every step.
5. A classmate was absent today. Write an explanation for them of how you found the height and why each step works. They will read it tomorrow without you there, so answer the questions you think they will ask.
6. List the assumptions your calculation makes about the tree or building, the ground, and your measurements. For each one, say whether breaking it would make your answer too high or too low, and describe a way to reduce its effect.

Example data, if the weather keeps you inside: distance 15 m, angle of elevation 38°, eye height 1.5 m.

Extension: from a second spot 25 m from the base, the angle of elevation is 27°. Find the height again. Do your two answers agree?

## Exit ticket

5 minutes.

A kite string is 40 meters long and makes a 55° angle with the ground. How high is the kite? Draw the triangle first.

## Answer key

For the teacher. Round lengths to the nearest hundredth of a meter unless noted.

Task A
- A3: height = 3.6 × sin(5°) = 0.31 m
- A4: flat distance = 3.6 × cos(5°) = 3.59 m. Check: 0.31² + 3.59² ≈ 12.98, and √12.98 ≈ 3.60, which matches the ramp length.
- A5: answers vary. A board that sags makes the true height slightly less than 0.31 m.

Task B, using the example data
- B4: height = 15 × tan(38°) + 1.5 = 6.15 m
- Extension: height = 25 × tan(27°) + 1.5 = 12.74 m

Exit ticket
- height = 40 × cos(55°) = 22.94 m
